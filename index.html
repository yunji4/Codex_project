      This happened

      In 2018 Reuters reported that Amazon had scrapped an experimental recruiting tool. It learned from ten years of resumes sent to the company, most of them from men. It taught itself to mark down resumes containing the word "women's", as in "women's chess club captain", and to downgrade graduates of two all-women's colleges.

      Engineers edited it to ignore those terms, but they couldn't promise it wouldn't find other ways to sort people by gender. Amazon says recruiters never used it to evaluate candidates. Read the Reuters story.

      The rule this page checks

      US hiring regulators use the four-fifths rule as a first test for discrimination. If one group gets selected at less than 80% of the rate of the most-selected group, that step of the hiring process gets a closer look.

      New York City's Local Law 144 requires a yearly bias audit for automated hiring tools, and those audits report this same impact ratio. Read the four-fifths rule and NYC's rules.

      How the simulation works

      Every applicant is made up. Men and women draw skill test scores and experience from the same distributions. Whether someone would do well in the job depends only on those two things plus some luck. The interview reveals that, so only people who would do well get an offer.

      Career breaks and the word "women's" have zero effect on job performance here, but women are more likely to have both on their resume. So every point of weight the model puts on them is a penalty on women that buys nothing. The pools are small, so a new pool can move the numbers around.
